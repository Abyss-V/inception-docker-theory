PID 1: signals, zombies, and why docker stop hangs

Inside a container your program is process number 1 — and the kernel treats process 1 unlike any other. Get this wrong and containers ignore shutdown, leak zombies, and die hard. Get it right in one line.

The idea in one sentence

When a container uses its own PID namespace, its main process is namespace PID 1. The kernel restricts which ordinary signals other processes may deliver to that namespace init, and orphaned descendants are adopted there unless a nearer subreaper exists — so shutdown and reaping must be designed deliberately.

1 Start here

Your process is PID 1, and that's unusual

Plain version: a container has its own process-number space, and your program is number 1 in it — the role the operating system's "init" normally plays.

From Lesson 6, a container has its own PID namespace, so your entrypoint isn't some ordinary process — it's PID 1, the init process of that namespace. On a normal Linux system PID 1 is a purpose-built init program (systemd, etc.) designed for the job. In a container, your app is unexpectedly cast in that role, and the kernel gives PID 1 two special behaviors that ordinary processes never deal with.

2 Special behavior one

Signals: namespace PID 1 only accepts ordinary signals it handles

Plain version: for namespace PID 1, an ordinary signal such as SIGTERM is deliverable from peer processes only if PID 1 installed a handler for it. An unhandled SIGTERM therefore does not terminate it.

For an ordinary process, SIGTERM has the default action “terminate.” The PID-namespace rule is narrower and more precise: processes in the same or an ancestor PID namespace may send an ordinary non-SIGKILL/SIGSTOP signal to namespace PID 1 only when PID 1 installed a handler for that signal. Therefore an unhandled SIGTERM does nothing. docker stop sends the configured stop signal, waits the configured timeout, and then sends SIGKILL. Ten seconds is the normal Linux default. It is configurable when the container is created with --stop-timeout; if unset, the daemon chooses the platform default.

Why the final SIGKILL always works. SIGKILL can't be caught by anyone, and while the kernel shields PID 1 from being killed by its own namespace, Docker's kill comes from the host namespace — an ancestor — which is not shielded. So the grace-period-then-SIGKILL path is guaranteed to stop the container, just ungracefully (abrupt exit, possible lost writes).
3 Special behavior two

Reaping: PID 1 must bury the dead

Plain version: when any process's parent dies, its leftover children get handed to PID 1, which must formally collect their exit status — or they pile up as "zombies."

When a process exits, it stays as a zombie (a defunct entry holding a slot in the process table) until its parent calls wait() to collect its exit status. If a parent dies before its children, those orphans are re-parented to PID 1. So PID 1 inherits the duty of reaping every orphan in the namespace. A real init does this in a loop; an app that never calls wait() on unexpected children doesn't — and if your PID 1 spawns subprocesses (or inherits orphans) without reaping, zombies accumulate and can exhaust the process table.

4 The trap

Shell form quietly makes sh your PID 1

Plain version: writing your entrypoint as a plain string hides a shell in front of your app — the shell becomes PID 1, and it's bad at the job.

Here's where Lesson 26's shell-vs-exec form bites. ENTRYPOINT node server.js becomes /bin/sh -c "node server.js", so sh is PID 1 and your app is its child. A shell normally waits for its own foreground child, but it is not automatically a general-purpose init or subreaper and usually does not forward SIGTERM to that child. The app can remain unsignaled, and unexpected orphaned descendants may go unreaped. Exec form puts the application directly at PID 1.

Shell form — sh is PID 1, signal stops here:

PID 1 /bin/sh -c "node server.js" ← docker stop's SIGTERM lands here, ignored/not forwarded └─ PID 7 node server.js ← your app, never told to shut down

Exec form — your app is PID 1:

PID 1 node server.js ← gets SIGTERM directly (if it handles it)
5 The fix

Write for PID 1, or delegate it to a tiny init

Plain version: either make your app handle SIGTERM and reap children, or run a minimal init that does both for you.

Two paths. If your app is PID 1 (exec form), give it a SIGTERM handler that shuts down cleanly, and reap children if it spawns any. But most apps aren't written to be init — so the common fix is to put a tiny init process at PID 1 that forwards signals and reaps zombies for you. tini is the standard one, and docker run --init injects it: tini becomes PID 1, your app runs as its child, and tini relays SIGTERM to your app and reaps any orphans. Graceful docker stop and zombie-free operation, for free.

With --init — tini is PID 1 and does the init duties:

PID 1 tini -- node server.js ← forwards SIGTERM ↓ , reaps zombies └─ PID 7 node server.js ← receives the forwarded signal, shuts down cleanly
The rule to remember

Namespace PID 1 receives ordinary peer/ancestor signals only when it installed a handler, and it is the final adopter for orphans unless a nearer subreaper exists. Use exec form, install a graceful stop-signal handler, and use --init/tini when the application is not designed to perform init duties.

Inspect it yourself

See who's PID 1, watch shell form eat the stop signal, then fix it with --init:

Check yourself

  • Your container's main process is PID 1 — the init of its PID namespace — and the kernel treats it specially.
  • For namespace PID 1, an ordinary peer/ancestor signal is deliverable only when PID 1 installed a handler; an unhandled SIGTERM therefore does nothing.
  • Orphans are re-parented to PID 1, which must reap them or zombies accumulate.
  • Shell form makes /bin/sh PID 1; it usually does not forward termination signals and is not guaranteed to reap arbitrary orphaned descendants. Exec form makes your app PID 1.
  • Fix: exec form + a real SIGTERM handler, or a tiny init (--init / tini) that forwards signals and reaps.
  • The final SIGKILL always works because it comes from the host namespace, not PID 1's own.
Where this corrects the familiar version

"docker stop is slow" is often PID 1 not handling the configured stop signal; the usual ten seconds is the Linux daemon default when the container has no configured stop timeout, not a fixed law. Shell form inserts sh as PID 1; it generally does not forward the termination signal and is not a full init/subreaper. The fix isn't a longer timeout — it's making the right process PID 1 (exec form, or tini) and handling the signal.

Where we are

Summary

When the container has its own PID namespace, the main process is namespace PID 1. Ordinary peer/ancestor signals are deliverable only when that init installed a handler, except that ancestor SIGKILL/SIGSTOP are forced; orphaned descendants are adopted by namespace PID 1 unless a nearer subreaper exists. So an app that ignores SIGTERM makes docker stop hang until the grace period expires and SIGKILL lands, and an app that never reaps leaks zombies. Shell form quietly inserts sh as PID 1 and breaks both; exec form puts your app there. The fixes are to handle SIGTERM yourself or delegate PID-1 duties to a tiny init like tini (--init).
