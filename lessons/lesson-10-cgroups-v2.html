cgroups v2: one tree to rule them all

v1 gave each controller its own separate tree, and that fragmentation caused real problems. v2 collapses them into a single unified hierarchy — which forces two new rules (explicit controller enabling, no processes in inner nodes) and renames the files. Same enforcement, cleaner design.

Lesson 9 ended on v1's defining awkwardness: every controller is its own tree, so a process is enrolled into each one separately and there's no single "the container's cgroup." cgroups v2 is the redesign that fixes this by merging everything into one hierarchy. That one change ripples out into everything else about v2 — how you turn controllers on, where processes are allowed to live, and even the file names. We'll take those in order, then look at the practical question of who owns the tree: the cgroupfs-vs-systemd driver choice.

Same source, same enforcement. The controllers and their behavior (memory kills, cpu throttles, pids refuses) are exactly as in Lesson 9 — v2 doesn't change what a limit does, only the interface. So this lesson focuses on the structural changes. Source is again the opencontainers/cgroups module's fs2/ package (the "2" is v2).

The intuition

Why merge the trees?

v1's separate-tree design let a process sit in the cpu controller's "group A" but the memory controller's "group B" — the groupings didn't have to agree. Three problems followed. There was no coherent notion of "this container's cgroup," only a scattered set of memberships. Controllers couldn't cooperate — memory pressure logic and I/O throttling live in different trees and can't easily coordinate. And safely handing a subtree to an unprivileged user (delegation) was basically impossible to reason about across N independent hierarchies.

v2's answer is blunt and effective: there is one tree. Every controller operates on the same set of cgroups. A cgroup is a single directory that carries all of its controllers' files side by side — memory.max, cpu.max, and pids.max in the same folder — and a process belongs to exactly one cgroup, period. That coherence is the whole point; the new rules below exist to make a single shared tree behave sanely.

Mechanism 1

One mount, one cgroup.procs for all controllers

Step 1 · Explain

v2 is a single filesystem mounted once at /sys/fs/cgroup — no per-controller subdirectories at the top. Placement still works by writing a PID into cgroup.procs, but now that one write enrolls the process under every controller enabled for that cgroup at once. Contrast v1, where the same PID had to be written into the cgroup.procs of the cpu tree, the memory tree, the pids tree, separately.

Mechanism 2

Turning controllers on: cgroup.subtree_control

Step 1 · Explain

With a single tree, a new question appears: at any given cgroup, which controllers are even active for its children? v2 answers with two files. cgroup.controllers (read-only) lists the controllers available in this cgroup. cgroup.subtree_control (writable) lists the controllers enabled for child cgroups — you turn one on by writing +cpu, off by writing -cpu. A controller is only usable in a child if every ancestor down to it has enabled that controller in its subtree_control. So creating a container's cgroup means walking down from the root, enabling the controllers you need at each level.

Step 2 · The source
The part that does the job

res becomes the string "+cpu +memory +pids +io", and writing it into cgroup.subtree_control is what "lights up" those controllers one level down. This explicit propagation is the price of a single tree: in v1 a controller was globally on because it had its own hierarchy; in v2 you must switch it on along the path. It's also the foundation of delegation — a parent grants a child exactly the controllers it wrote into subtree_control, and no more.

Mechanism 3

The rule that surprises everyone: no processes in inner nodes

Step 1 · Explain

Here's a constraint v1 never had. In v2, a cgroup that has controllers enabled for its children may not itself directly contain processes — only the root cgroup is exempt. Processes live only in leaf cgroups. Why? Imagine a cgroup that holds some loose processes and several child cgroups, all under the memory controller. The controller would have to decide how to split memory between the loose processes and the child groups — an ambiguous, policy-laden question. v2 removes the ambiguity by fiat: a cgroup is either a place processes live or a place that distributes resources to children, never both. This is the "no internal processes" rule, and it's exactly why runc's creation code has to fuss over cgroup "types" (domain/threaded):

Step 2 · The source
The part that does the job

The "domain invalid" state is the kernel telling runc "this cgroup has a process directly in it, so I won't let you enable resource controllers on its children." The whole switch exists to detect and cope with that. The practical takeaway for you: in v2, your container's processes sit in a leaf cgroup (e.g. docker-<id>.scope), and the parent slices above it hold no processes of their own — they only apportion resources downward.

Mechanism 4

The files got renamed (and cpu got simpler)

Step 1 · Explain

The controllers do the same things as Lesson 9, but v2 cleaned up their interface. Most notable: the two CPU throttle files became one. In v1 you wrote quota and period into separate files; in v2 you write both into a single file cpu.max as "quota period" — for example "50000 100000" for half a CPU, or "max 100000" for uncapped.

Mechanism 5

Who owns the tree: cgroupfs vs systemd driver

Step 1 · Explain

One more decision, and it's a source of real-world breakage if you get it wrong. Something has to actually create the container's cgroup directory and write its limits. There are two "drivers" for doing that. The cgroupfs driver: runc writes the cgroup files directly — mkdir the directory, echo the limits, as we've seen. Simple. But on a host running systemd, systemd also considers itself the manager of the cgroup tree, and will periodically reorganize or clean up cgroups it doesn't know about — potentially yanking the rug from under Docker. Two managers, one tree, conflict.

The systemd driver avoids the fight by not touching the files at all. Instead, runc asks systemd — over its D-Bus API — to create a transient unit (a "scope") for the container. systemd creates and owns the cgroup, and translates unit properties like MemoryMax and CPUQuota into the very same cgroup files. systemd stays the single source of truth for the tree.

Step 2 · The source
The part that does the job

Both drivers end at the same cgroup files — the difference is who writes them. cgroupfs: runc, directly. systemd: systemd, on runc's request via StartTransientUnit, so there's one owner. Docker's setting is native.cgroupdriver=systemd (vs cgroupfs) in /etc/docker/daemon.json. The rule of thumb: on a systemd host, use the systemd driver — and in Kubernetes it must match the kubelet's driver, or the two disagree about the tree and containers fail to start. This is why docker-<id>.scope and system.slice appear in the v2 paths: those are systemd unit names.

Where this corrects the familiar version

People often think "cgroups v2 is just v1 with renamed files." The renames are the least of it. The substance is structural: one hierarchy instead of many, controllers that must be explicitly enabled down the tree via subtree_control, and the hard no-internal-processes rule that forces your processes into leaf cgroups. Those constraints are why v2 can do coherent, delegatable resource control that v1 couldn't. And the driver choice is not cosmetic: mismatching cgroupfs/systemd between Docker and systemd (or kubelet) is a classic cause of containers that won't start or limits that silently don't stick.

Where we are

Summary

cgroups v2 replaces v1's many per-controller trees with a single unified hierarchy mounted at /sys/fs/cgroup, where each cgroup directory carries all its controllers' files and one write to cgroup.procs enrolls a process everywhere at once. The single tree brings two new rules: controllers must be turned on for children by writing +cpu +memory … into cgroup.subtree_control, and only leaf cgroups may hold processes (the "no internal processes" rule, visible in runc's domain/threaded handling). The controllers behave exactly as in Lesson 9; only the files changed — most notably cpu.max now holds "quota period" together, and memory.limit_in_bytes became memory.max. Finally, the cgroupfs-vs-systemd driver decides who writes those files — runc directly, or systemd via a transient unit over D-Bus — and on systemd hosts the systemd driver keeps a single owner of the tree. That completes the resource-limit primitives. Next, Part II's last strand: the security primitives — starting with capabilities.
