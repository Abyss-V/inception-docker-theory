ChainID, fully: what it is, who builds it, when, and why

Lesson 17 gave you the formula but not the story. This fills in the missing part: who computes a ChainID, at what moment, and what it's actually for — grounded in the containerd source.

The idea in one sentence

A ChainID is the single fingerprint of a whole stack of layers applied in order — the name Docker gives to "the filesystem you get after stacking layers 0 through N."

1 The problem it solves

Why a diffID isn't enough

Plain version: a diffID names one layer. But the thing Docker stores and reuses is a stack of layers already applied together — and that needs its own name.

Recall two facts. From Lesson 17: a diffID is the fingerprint of one layer's content, on its own. From Lesson 16: what the snapshotter actually stores is a committed snapshot — a real, materialized filesystem that is the result of stacking several layers.

Here's the catch that makes diffID insufficient. Take one layer — say "add nginx." Apply it on top of Alpine, and you get one filesystem. Apply the exact same layer on top of Ubuntu, and you get a different filesystem. Same layer, same diffID — but two different results, because what's underneath differs. So the snapshot (a result-of-stacking) can't be named by any single layer's diffID; it has to be named by something that captures the whole ordered stack. That something is the ChainID.

The crux

diffID = the identity of a layer. ChainID = the identity of a stack of layers applied in order. A snapshot is a stack's result, so snapshots are keyed by ChainID, never by diffID.

2 The ingredients

What goes in: the list of diffIDs

Plain version: the input is just the image's ordered list of layer diffIDs — the same list from Lesson 17.

An image's config carries an ordered list of its layers' diffIDs (in the field rootfs.diff_ids — you'll meet the config properly in Lesson 18). That ordered list is the entire raw material. ChainIDs are computed from nothing but those diffIDs, in order. No file contents are re-read; it's pure hashing of the fingerprints you already have.

3 The recipe

How it's folded together, step by step

Plain version: start with the first layer's diffID; then repeatedly hash "what you have so far" together with "the next layer's diffID." Each step swallows the whole history.

The rule (from the OCI identity.ChainIDs() function, which containerd vendors and uses) is a running fold. Write D0, D1, D2… for the diffIDs and C0, C1, C2… for the ChainIDs after each layer:

The key thing the diagram shows: each ChainID is hashed from the previous ChainID (not the previous diffID) plus the next diffID. That's what makes it "swallow the history" — C2 can only be produced by exactly D0, then D1, then D2, in that order. Change any layer, or the order, and every ChainID from that point on changes.

4 Who and when

The unpacker computes it, at pull time

Plain version: containerd's "unpacker" does this while it's turning a freshly-pulled image into on-disk snapshots — not runc, not at container start.

The computation happens in one specific place: containerd's unpacker (core/unpack/unpacker.go), which runs when an image is pulled and its layers are being unpacked into snapshots. It calls identity.ChainIDs(diffIDs) to turn the diffID list into the running list of ChainIDs, then walks the layers bottom-to-top: for each layer it takes that layer's ChainID, and unpacks the layer's tar onto a snapshot keyed by that ChainID. So by the time the image is fully unpacked, there is one committed snapshot per prefix of the stack, each named by its ChainID. This is a pull/unpack-time activity, done once — not something that recomputes every time you start a container.

5 What it's used for

The snapshot key: dedup and the container's parent

Plain version: the ChainID is the snapshot's name, so Docker uses it two ways — to check "do I already have this stack?" (skip work) and to attach a container's writable layer onto the right image rootfs.

Because each snapshot is keyed by its ChainID, two operations become trivial. First, dedup / cache-hit: before unpacking, containerd does a snapshotter.Stat(ChainID) (client/image.go) — if a snapshot with that ChainID already exists, the stack is already on disk and unpacking is skipped entirely. Two images sharing their first three layers produce identical C0, C1, C2, so the second image reuses the first's snapshots for free. Second, running a container: the image's full rootfs is the committed snapshot keyed by the top ChainID, so container creation computes identity.ChainID(diffIDs) and calls snapshotter.Prepare(containerID, parent=thatChainID) (client/container_opts.go) — attaching the container's fresh writable layer (the active snapshot from Lesson 16) on top of the image identified by its ChainID.

Check yourself

  • A diffID names one layer; a ChainID names a whole ordered stack of layers applied together.
  • ChainID is needed because a snapshot is the result of stacking — the same layer on two different bases gives two different results, so it can't be named by a single diffID.
  • It's a running fold: C0 = D0; each next ChainID = sha256(previous ChainID + " " + next diffID), so it bakes in the whole ordered history.
  • It's computed by containerd's unpacker at pull time (via identity.ChainIDs), once — not at container start.
  • It's used as the snapshot key: a Stat(ChainID) hit means "already unpacked" (dedup), and a container's writable layer is Prepared with the image's top ChainID as its parent.
  • Shared layer prefixes → identical ChainIDs → the unpacked stack is stored once and reused across images.
